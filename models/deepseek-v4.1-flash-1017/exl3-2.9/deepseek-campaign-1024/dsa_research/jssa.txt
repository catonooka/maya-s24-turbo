 Abstract 
 
 Sparse attention reduces the KV-cache traffic that dominates long-context LLM decoding, but practical methods largely operate at coarse granularity.
Token-level selection offers finer control, yet presents two challenges: low-dimensional scoring may lose query–key structure needed for accurate ranking, while gathering scattered selected tokens can offset the resulting latency benefit.
We introduce Joint Subspace Sparse Attention (JSSA) to address both challenges.
For selection quality, JSSA learns a shared low-dimensional query–key subspace by minimizing token-score preservation error.
We upper-bound the resulting non-convex objective with a tractable marginal objective that admits a closed-form spectral solution; direct refinement of the exact objective provides no measurable improvement in the tested settings.
For execution efficiency, we develop a gather-free token-level indexed-attention kernel that supports distinct per-KV-head selections under the standard grouped-query attention (GQA) KV-cache layout.
Across standard GQA, hybrid sliding-window/global attention, and Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) models, JSSA consistently remains close to full-KV accuracy while avoiding the substantial generation-length inflation and architecture-dependent failures observed with competing methods.
On an NVIDIA B200 GPU, JSSA achieves up to 6.43 × the decoding throughput of full-KV attention at 128K context.
JSSA achieves reasoning accuracy comparable to model-native DSA without model training and reaches 96–99% of its decoding throughput even at 700B scale large models. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 1 Introduction 

 
 Long-context autoregressive decoding is often bottlenecked by repeatedly accessing the growing KV cache.
With full attention, each decoding step scores the current query against the entire context and reads all the corresponding values, causing memory traffic to grow linearly with sequence length.
Because attention is typically memory-bandwidth-bound in this regime, reducing KV accesses is one of the most direct ways to accelerate long-context decoding.
Sparse attention reduces KV access by selecting only part of the context at each decoding step.
Block- or page-level methods  ( Tang et al., 2024 ; Gao et al., 2026 ) align naturally with paged KV-cache layouts and block-sparse kernels, whereas token-level methods  ( Wu et al., 2025 ; Ribar et al., 2024 ) such as Loki  ( Singhania et al., 2024 ) and FASA  ( Wang et al., 2026 ) select individual positions.
Token-level selection can represent finer-grained sparse patterns, yet this flexibility has not translated reliably into either higher accuracy or lower latency. 
 
 
 Figure 1: 
 JSSA at one decoding step for a single KV head. 
The mean of its associated query heads scores a projected-key cache in a shared low-dimensional subspace to select the top- B token positions.
The query heads share this index set, and gather-free indexed attention accesses the corresponding original full-dimensional keys and values.
 
 
 
 This finer granularity creates two challenges.
First, objectives that preserve queries or keys independently may not preserve the bilinear scores used for token ranking.
Second, token-level selection produces irregular per-KV-head index sets in grouped-query attention (GQA).
Gathering them into a contiguous buffer adds overhead, and forcing all KV heads to share one selected set can degrade accuracy. 
 
 
 We introduce Joint Subspace Sparse Attention (JSSA), a training-free method that addresses both challenges.
For selection, JSSA learns a shared query–key subspace by minimizing bilinear score error through a closed-form spectral surrogate; directly refining the exact objective provides no measurable selection benefit in the tested settings.
For execution, JSSA preserves distinct per-KV-head token sets and consumes them directly through a gather-free indexed-attention kernel under the standard GQA KV-cache layout.
Together, these components provide an end-to-end token-level sparse-decoding path across GQA and MLA  ( Liu et al., 2024 ) models. 
 
 
 Our main contributions are: 
 
 • 
 
 Score-preserving joint query–key projection. 
We formulate an exact bilinear score-error objective, derive a tractable marginal upper bound with a closed-form spectral solution, and show that direct refinement provides no measurable selection benefit in the tested settings. 
 
 • 
 
 Gather-free per-KV-head token-level attention for GQA. 
We design, to the best of our knowledge, the first indexed-attention kernel that supports distinct per-KV-head selections under the standard GQA KV-cache layout without physically gathering selected entries. 
 
 • 
 
 Validation across architectures and scales. 
JSSA remains close to full-KV accuracy across GQA, hybrid local/global attention, and MLA models, and achieves up to 6.43 × the decoding throughput of full-KV attention at 128K context on a B200 GPU.
We further show that JSSA attains reasoning accuracy comparable to model-native DSA  ( DeepSeek-AI, 2025 ) on 700B-parameter scale models, without any model training. 
 
 
 
 
 
 2 Motivation 

 
 2.1 Low-Dimensional Token Scoring Requires the Right Objective 

 
 Token-level selection must score every candidate token.
Unlike page-level methods, which reduce cost along the sequence axis by assigning one score to each page, token-level methods retain every position as a candidate and must instead reduce the channel dimension used for scoring.
They may exploit part of original channels, as in FASA, or project the scoring vectors onto an arbitrary low-rank subspace, as in Loki. 
 
 
 Discrete channel selection is a restricted form of orthonormal projection, since retaining r original channels corresponds to choosing r standard basis vectors.
An arbitrary projection therefore offers greater flexibility, but this flexibility alone does not guarantee more accurate token ranking.
The subspace must be chosen using an objective that preserves the query–key interactions that determine attention scores.
Figure  2 (a) demonstrates this distinction: FASA’s discrete channel selection outperforms Loki’s key-reconstruction projection at every evaluated rank, with particularly large gaps at tight budgets.
The comparison motivates choosing the scoring subspace to preserve query–key interactions rather than key representations alone.

(b) Gather-then-attend latency at 128K context, normalized to dense attention, with a breakdown of the residual sparse-attention cost.
 
 
 
 
 2.2 Gather Overhead Limits Token-Level Speedup 

 
 Even accurate token selection faces a separate systems challenge.
Fine-grained selection can produce irregular index sets that do not map naturally to the layouts supported by conventional attention kernels.
A common implementation therefore gathers the selected key-value entries into a contiguous buffer before invoking a dense attention kernel.
Figure  2 (b) shows this overhead on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct  ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ) at 128K context by comparing gather-then-attend with reduced attention over the same selected tokens when the KV entries are already contiguous.
Although sparsity substantially lowers total latency, gathering remains comparable to the reduced-attention computation itself and accounts for more than half of the residual latency at batch size 16.
Thus, physically materializing selected KV entries can erase a substantial fraction of the benefit enabled by token-level sparsity. 
 
 
 These observations motivate two complementary requirements: a low-dimensional scoring subspace that preserves token rankings, and an attention kernel that directly accesses the selected KV entries. 
 
 
 
 
 3 Joint Subspace Sparse Attention 

 
 3.1 Overview 

 
 JSSA is a training-free token-level sparse-attention method for GQA and MLA, as illustrated in Figure  1 .
For each layer and KV head, it maintains a low-dimensional projected-key cache 𝐊 proj , h alongside the original KV cache.
At each decoding step, JSSA projects the mean query 𝐪 ¯ t , h of the G query heads associated with KV head h , scores it against 𝐊 proj , h , and selects the top- B token positions.
The associated query heads share this per-KV-head index set, while attention is computed from the original full-dimensional queries, keys, and values.
For GQA, our gather-free indexed kernel directly accesses the selected KV entries; for MLA, we use FlashMLA’s sparse attention kernel  ( Li et al., 2025 ) . 
 
 
 
 3.2 Joint Query–Key Subspace 

 
 For each layer and KV head, JSSA uses an orthonormal basis
 M ∈ ℝ r × d with M ​ M ⊤ = I r , and applies the same basis to both queries and keys.
Let P = M ⊤ ​ M be the corresponding rank- r projector.
Unlike asymmetric query–key factorizations such as KQ-SVD  ( Lesens et al., 2025 ) , JSSA constrains both operands to a single shared scoring subspace; Appendix  B.6 evaluates an asymmetric KQ-SVD factorization adapted to the same token-selection pipeline.
In GQA, selection uses the mean query
 q ¯ = 1 G ​ ∑ g = 1 G q g 
of the query heads associated with the KV head.
The projected score is 
 

 
 
 s ^ = q ¯ ⊤ ​ P ​ k , 
 
 (1) 
 
 and the resulting score error is 
 

 
 
 s − s ^ = q ¯ ⊤ ​ ( I − P ) ​ k . 
 
 (2) 
 
 
 
 We therefore choose P to minimize the expected squared error of the bilinear score used for token ranking.
Unlike query- or key-reconstruction objectives, this objective directly preserves the query–key interaction that determines token ordering.
For GQA, it is defined over the group-mean query used for per-KV-head selection. 
 
 
 Let G ~ Q ¯ and G ~ K denote the independently unit-trace-normalized second-moment matrices of the calibration group-mean queries and keys, preventing either marginal from dominating the surrogate.
The exact selection objective is 
 

 
 
 ℒ sel ​ ( P ) = tr ⁡ ( ( I − P ) ​ G ~ Q ¯ ​ ( I − P ) ​ G ~ K ) . 
 
 (3) 
 
 
 
 The exact objective is non-convex and has no general closed-form solution.
For any orthogonal projector P , however, 
 

 
 
 ℒ sel ​ ( P ) ≤ 1 4 ​ ℒ marg ​ ( P ) 2 , ℒ marg ​ ( P ) = tr ⁡ ( ( I − P ) ​ ( G ~ Q ¯ + G ~ K ) ) . 
 
 (4) 
 
 The full derivation is provided in Appendix  B.3 .
The marginal objective admits the closed-form solution 
 

 
 
 M ⋆ = TopEig r ⁡ ( G ~ Q ¯ + G ~ K ) ⊤ , 
 
 (5) 
 
 where TopEig r ⁡ ( A ) returns the top- r eigenvectors of A as columns.
The bound provides a tractable surrogate but does not establish tightness or global optimality.
Direct optimization from M ⋆ produces negligible subspace changes and no measurable improvement in oracle top- B recall across the tested settings (Appendix  B.4 ).
We therefore use M ⋆ directly, reducing calibration to second-moment accumulation followed by one eigendecomposition per layer and KV head. 
 
 
 
 3.3 Gather-Free Indexed Attention 

 
 After selecting the top- B positions for each KV head, JSSA computes attention directly from their scattered locations in the original full-dimensional KV cache. Irregular indices are not themselves a barrier at this stage: a single token’s key or value slice is already contiguous over head dim, so each indexed read remains a burst-sized transaction and irregularity only increases the number of transactions rather than degrading each one.
The main challenge in GQA lies instead in the cache layout, where each KV head occupies a separate partition and may select a different token set. Collapsing selections into a single layer-shared set reduces accuracy at long context (Appendix  E.3 ). This issue does not arise in MLA, where each token contributes one shared latent vector per layer and selection produces a single index set, the regime targeted by the released FlashMLA sparse kernels. 
 
 
 We therefore develop a Triton indexed-attention kernel that, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to support gather-free token-level attention with distinct per-KV-head selections under the standard GQA KV-cache layout. It launches once per layer and resolves the per-head index sets internally, mapping each query head to the indices selected by its associated KV head and accessing the corresponding keys and values in place. This avoids both the staging buffer required by gather-then-attend and the per-KV-head launches otherwise needed to keep distinct selections.
Token scoring still examines all N candidates, whereas attention accesses only the selected B positions; Section  4.7 shows consistent end-to-end speedups over gather-then-attend. 
 
 
 
 3.4 Projected Scoring Cache 

 
 JSSA maintains
 𝐊 proj = 𝐊 ​ M ⊤ 
as a scoring-only auxiliary cache for token selection, while final attention uses the original full-dimensional keys and values.
For GQA models, we choose r so that the FP8 projected-key cache occupies at most 6.25 % of the original BF16 KV cache.
For MLA, we use a 128-dimensional scoring representation comprising 64 latent and 64 RoPE dimensions, matching the total dimensionality of the DSA indexer for an equal-capacity comparison.
We use FP8 throughout; Appendix  E.2 verifies negligible accuracy impact relative to BF16. 
 
 
 
 
 4 Experiments 

 
 4.1 Setup 

 
 We evaluate JSSA across standard GQA, hybrid local/global GQA, and MLA architectures.
LongBench  ( Bai et al., 2024 ) , RULER  ( Hsieh et al., 2024 ) , and PG-19  ( Rae et al., 2019 ) use LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-14B  ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , and Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512  ( Liu et al., 2026 ) .
Reasoning and throughput experiments use Qwen3-8B, Gemma4-26B-A4B-It  ( Gemma Team et al., 2026 ) , and GLM-4.7-Flash  ( GLM Team et al., 2025 ) , respectively representing GQA, hybrid local/global GQA, and MLA.
For Gemma4, JSSA is applied only to the global-attention layers, while local layers retain the native sliding-window attention.
We compare against full-KV and the supported sparse-attention baselines for each architecture; complete model coverage, decoding configurations, and implementation details are provided in Appendix  A .
For GQA models, we set the FP8 projected-key cache to at most 6.25% of the original BF16 KV cache ( r = 32 for LLaMA, Qwen, and Ministral; r = 64 for Gemma4); for MLA, we use a 128-dimensional scoring representation matching the total dimensionality of the DSA indexer.

 4.4 Reasoning 

 
 Table  3 reports reasoning performance at a KV budget of 1,024 tokens across standard GQA, hybrid local/global GQA, and MLA models.
Each entry includes pass@1 accuracy and generation length relative to full-KV, since degraded sparse attention can destabilize reasoning trajectories even when the final answer remains correct.
Across architectures, JSSA provides the strongest overall accuracy–length trade-off, remaining within 1.3 points of full-KV accuracy on most benchmarks while limiting generation-length inflation to 2–17% on Qwen3-8B and Gemma4-26B-A4B-It, versus substantially longer generations for TriAttention  ( Mao et al., 2026 ) , QUEST, and SeerAttention-R.

Complete results across budgets of 512, 1,024, and 2,048 tokens are provided in Appendix  E.5 .

 4.5 Decoding Throughput 

 
 We measure end-to-end decoding throughput on a single NVIDIA B200 GPU across Qwen3-8B, Gemma4-26B-A4B-It, and GLM-4.7-Flash, covering GQA, hybrid sliding-window/global attention, and MLA architectures.
All sparse methods use a KV budget of B = 2,048 tokens.
We sweep batch size at 32K and 128K context using the FP8 projected scoring cache and the gather-free indexed-attention kernel described in Sections  3.4 and  3.3 . 
 
 
 Figure  4 reports the resulting throughput.
JSSA consistently improves over full-KV, and its advantage generally grows at 128K as KV-cache traffic becomes more dominant.
At 128K, JSSA reaches up to 1.51 × , 1.88 × , and 6.43 × the throughput of full-KV on Qwen3-8B, Gemma4-26B-A4B-It, and GLM-4.7-Flash, respectively.
On GLM-4.7-Flash, JSSA matches FASA’s throughput while providing substantially higher reasoning accuracy, indicating that the joint scoring objective adds little overhead (complete sweeps in Appendix  D ). 
 
 
 
 4.6 Validation at Large Scale 

 
 We evaluate JSSA on two model families at 700B-parameter scale, DeepSeek-V3 and GLM-5.2, equipped with model-native DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA).
This allows us to compare post-hoc JSSA calibration against sparse-attention mechanisms introduced during model training.
For DeepSeek, we apply JSSA to DeepSeek-V3.1-Terminus (full attention) and compare against the officially released DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp, which introduces native DSA through continued pre-training from
the Terminus.
For GLM-5.2, an independently developed MLA model, JSSA follows the model’s native schedule of invoking selection only at a subset of layers and reusing indices across adjacent layers; no full-attention checkpoint is available for this model.
Complete experimental details are provided in Appendix  C .

 4.7 Ablation Studies 

 
 Figure 5: 
 Scoring-subspace ablations. 
(a) LongBench accuracy versus projection rank on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct at a KV budget of 256 tokens.
(b) PG-19 perplexity on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct under the same fixed-density KV budgets used in the main evaluation, for bases calibrated on different corpora and context lengths.
Calibration transfers across datasets, but bases calibrated at 32K degrade when evaluated at 128K.
 
 
 
 Projection rank. 

 
 Figure  5 (a) compares JSSA with FASA and Loki as the scoring dimension varies from 4 to 32. JSSA performs best at every rank, with the largest gains under the tightest constraints (e.g., + 5.40 and + 24.48 LongBench points over FASA and Loki at r = 4 ), showing that the joint query–key objective uses limited scoring capacity more effectively. 
 
 
 
 Calibration robustness. 

 
 Figure  5 (b) separates the effects of calibration corpus and calibration length under the same fixed-density KV budgets used in the main evaluation.
JSSA is largely insensitive to the calibration corpus: bases calibrated on LongAlign, NarrativeQA, and LooGLE yield nearly identical perplexity at both 32K and 128K evaluation.
Calibration length matters more: bases calibrated at 32K degrade sharply when evaluated at 128K (perplexity 16.05–18.31, versus 11.31–11.48 for 128K-calibrated bases), indicating that calibration should cover sequences near the maximum deployment context length. 
 
 
 
 Gather-free execution. 

 
 Figure  6 isolates the benefit of consuming the selected KV entries directly through indexed attention rather than first materializing them into a contiguous buffer, using the same selected token sets with a KV budget of B = 2,048 tokens.
At 128K context on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, gather-free execution roughly doubles the attention-stage speedup over full-KV attention at batch sizes 4 and 16 (e.g., 25.35 × → 65.26 × at batch 16).
On Gemma4-26B-A4B-It, it improves end-to-end throughput by 1.07 × – 2.22 × over gather-then-attend, with the gain growing with batch size, showing that physically gathering selected KV entries remains a substantial overhead even after sparsification.

 5 Related Work 

 
 Coarse-grained sparse attention. 

 
 Coarse-grained methods amortize selection and execution over groups of tokens.
Page- and block-level approaches like QUEST  ( Tang et al., 2024 ) and SeerAttention-R  ( Gao et al., 2026 ) estimate query-dependent importance for KV pages that map naturally to block-sparse kernels.
For prefill, MInference  ( Jiang et al., 2024 ) , FlexPrefill  ( Lai et al., 2025 ) , and XAttention  ( Xu et al., 2025 ) identify input-dependent sparse patterns to reduce prompt-processing cost.
Model-native designs such as NSA  ( Yuan et al., 2025 ) and MSA  ( MiniMax, 2026 ) jointly train sparse selection and hardware-aligned execution across prefill and decoding.
SqueezedAttention  ( Hooper et al., 2025 ) instead retrieves representation-similar clusters via centroids. Because block- and cluster-level methods may access irrelevant members within selected groups, JSSA performs individual-token selection instead. 
 
 
 
 Token-level sparse attention and execution. 

 
 Dynamic token-level methods reconsider individual KV positions for each decoding query.
SparQ  ( Ribar et al., 2024 ) uses salient query channels, TokenSelect  ( Wu et al., 2025 ) aggregates head-specific token preferences, Loki  ( Singhania et al., 2024 ) scores tokens in a key-reconstruction subspace, and FASA  ( Wang et al., 2026 ) selects original RoPE channels.
SALS  ( Mu et al., 2025 ) retrieves tokens from a compressed latent KV cache, while model-native DSA  DeepSeek-AI (2025) learns a low-dimensional token indexer during training.
Related KV-reduction methods, including H 2 O  ( Zhang et al., 2023 ) , SnapKV  ( Li et al., 2024 ) , and TriAttention  ( Mao et al., 2026 ) , permanently evict token-level entries rather than dynamically retrieving from the complete cache.
JSSA retains the complete cache, derives its scoring space from joint query–key score-preservation error, and preserves distinct selected sets across KV heads.
Existing efficient execution paths primarily target regular block selections or MLA’s shared latent cache, including the sparse kernels released in FlashMLA  ( Li et al., 2025 ) .
JSSA instead provides gather-free indexed attention for irregular per-KV-head selections under the standard GQA KV-cache layout. 
 
 
 
 Projected and low-rank attention representations. 

 
 Low-dimensional transformations have also been used to reduce KV storage or final-attention computation.
EigenAttention  ( Saxena et al., 2024 ) evaluates attention in a reduced-dimensional space, KVTC  ( Staniszewski and Lancucki, 2026 ) applies a decorrelating transform before compression, and SWAN  ( Prakash et al., 2025 ) rotates and prunes KV features using an orthogonal transformation.
KQ-SVD  ( Lesens et al., 2025 ) instead derives asymmetric query and key projections that directly target attention-score fidelity; Section  3 and Appendix  B.6 compare this asymmetric parameterization with JSSA’s shared scoring subspace.
These methods replace or compress the representation consumed by final attention, whereas JSSA retains the original full-dimensional KV cache and uses projection only for an auxiliary scoring cache. 
 
 
 
 
 6 Conclusion 

 
 We presented JSSA, a training-free token-level sparse-attention method that jointly addresses selection quality and execution efficiency in long-context decoding.
JSSA derives a shared query–key scoring subspace from a closed-form spectral objective and eliminates token-gathering overhead through gather-free indexed attention with per-KV-head selections in GQA.
Across GQA, hybrid local/global attention, and MLA models, JSSA remains close to full-KV accuracy while delivering substantial throughput gains.
At approximately 700B scale, JSSA matches model-native DSA’s reasoning accuracy while reaching 96–99% of its decoding throughput, without model training.

 A.3 JSSA Configuration 

 
 For GQA models, JSSA maintains one projected-key scoring cache for each layer and KV head.
The query heads associated with a KV head share the token set selected using their mean projected query.
We use r = 32 for LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512, and r = 64 for the global-attention layers of Gemma4-26B-A4B-It.
With FP8 storage, these projected-key caches occupy at most 6.25 % of the corresponding BF16 KV caches. 
 
 
 For MLA models, JSSA uses a 128-dimensional scoring representation comprising 64 latent and 64 RoPE dimensions, matching the total dimensionality used by DSA.
Selection operates over the shared latent cache and therefore produces one layer-level token set rather than distinct per-KV-head selections.
Sparse attention is executed using FlashMLA. 
 
 
 Across all architectures, the projected scoring cache is stored in FP8, while final sparse attention uses the original full-dimensional BF16 KV representations.
We apply the same token budget to every sparse-attention layer and head.
JSSA does not reserve sink tokens, enforce a recent-token window, or apply special handling to any token positions.
For Gemma4, only the global-attention layers are sparsified; the local layers retain the model’s native sliding-window attention.
Appendix  E.2 evaluates the accuracy impact of scoring-cache quantization. 
 
 
 
 A.4 JSSA Calibration 

 
 JSSA is calibrated without updating any model parameters.
Because individual LongAlign-10K samples extend only to approximately 64K tokens, using them directly would not expose the projection bases to the 128K-context attention patterns targeted in our evaluation.
We therefore filter the corpus to documents containing at least 8K tokens, concatenate consecutive documents in corpus order until reaching at least 150K tokens, and center-crop the result to 131,072 tokens.
We construct eight calibration sequences using starting offsets separated by five documents; the resulting sequences may overlap because each concatenated window can span more than five documents. 
 
 
 For each sequence, we run a dense prefill pass and accumulate the second moments of group-mean queries and keys separately for every layer and KV head.
We then compute one projection basis per layer and KV head using the closed-form solution in Equation  20 .
For Gemma4, calibration is performed only for the global-attention layers, while the local sliding-window layers remain unchanged.
No gradient-based optimization, model training, or downstream evaluation data is used.

 A.6 Baseline Implementations 

 
 We evaluate Loki, FASA, and QUEST within the same vLLM-based serving stack as JSSA.
SqueezedAttention, SnapKV, SeerAttention-R, and TriAttention are integrated into Hugging Face Transformers from their official codebases.
We evaluate each baseline only where its original formulation can be transferred without substantial method-specific design choices. 
 
 
 Loki. 

 
 Loki shares JSSA’s projected scoring cache, top- B selection pipeline, and gather-free indexed-attention backend, differing only in its key-reconstruction projection objective.
We match its projection rank, cache precision, token budget, and execution path to JSSA, isolating the effect of the projection objective. 
 
 
 
 FASA. 

 
 We integrate FASA’s discrete channel-selection and scoring procedure into our vLLM-based serving stack.
Because FASA does not specify the selection granularity for GQA, we perform scoring and token selection independently for each KV head using the mean of its associated query heads.
We use the same scoring dimension and token budget as JSSA.
For each model, FASA is calibrated once on the original, unmodified LongAlign examples, and the resulting calibration is reused across benchmarks and context lengths without benchmark-specific recalibration or tuning.
Appendix  A.5 compares plain and packed calibration inputs and motivates this fixed choice.
Following FASA’s deployment design, selected channels are read directly from the original key cache rather than stored in a separate projected scoring cache. 
 
 
 
 QUEST. 

 
 We adapt QUEST from MHA to GQA by performing page scoring and selection independently for each KV head.
The open page containing the most recent tokens is always retained, following QUEST’s native paged-cache behavior.
To preserve vLLM’s standard KV-cache layout while supporting distinct selected page sets, we launch the sparse-attention kernel separately for each KV head. 
 
 
 
 MLA baselines. 

 
 Most existing baselines do not define a direct mapping to MLA’s shared latent cache, and adapting them would require additional choices in scoring representation and selection semantics.
We therefore implement only FASA as a representative post-hoc baseline, applying it to all 64 channels of the RoPE cache.
This already uses half of the 128-dimensional scoring representation used by JSSA and DSA. 
 
 
 
 Other baselines. 

 
 SqueezedAttention and SeerAttention-R retain their official coarse-grained selection procedures and recommended model-specific configurations.
SeerAttention-R additionally uses its released model-specific trained checkpoint and is therefore evaluated only on Qwen3-8B.
SnapKV and TriAttention perform token-level KV eviction rather than per-step dynamic retrieval; we retain their official eviction procedures and recommended configurations. 
 
 
 
 
 A.7 Hardware and Measurement Protocol 

 
 Unless otherwise stated, system experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA B200 GPU with tensor parallelism degree one using our vLLM-based implementation.
CUDA graph capture is enabled, and each run generates 128 new tokens from the target input context.
For each configuration, we perform one warm-up run followed by three measured runs and report the mean aggregate decoding throughput. 
 
 
 Measurements include projected-score computation, top- B selection, sparse attention, and the remaining model execution during decoding.
They exclude model loading, JSSA calibration, kernel compilation, and warm-up.
The original full-dimensional KV cache is stored in BF16, while JSSA’s projected scoring cache is stored in FP8. 
 
 
 
 
 Appendix B Joint Subspace Objective Details 

 
 B.1 Product-of-Marginals Formulation 

 
 Let 𝒟 Q ¯ and 𝒟 K denote the empirical marginals of group-mean queries and keys collected during calibration.
We define the selection objective over independent draws 
 

 
 
 q ¯ ∼ 𝒟 Q ¯ , k ∼ 𝒟 K , 
 
 (6) 
 
 or equivalently over the product distribution 𝒟 Q ¯ × 𝒟 K . 
 
 
 This formulation reflects the runtime use of the selector, where each decoding query is scored against every key in the context rather than only against the key observed at the same sequence position.
The calibration objective therefore measures the projected-score error q ¯ ⊤ ​ ( I − P ) ​ k across the query–key combinations that may be compared during inference.
Appendix  B.2 defines the exact selection objective as the second moment of this error after trace normalization. 
 
 
 Because query–key pairs are drawn from the product distribution, q ¯ and k are independent by construction.
Thus, for any functions f and g , 
 

 
 
 𝔼 ⁡ [ f ⁡ ( q ¯ ) ​ g ​ ( k ) ] = 𝔼 ⁡ [ f ⁡ ( q ¯ ) ] ​ 𝔼 ​ [ g ⁡ ( k ) ] . 
 
 (7) 
 
 Since trace normalization rescales each marginal by a constant, the same factorization holds for the rescaled activations in Appendix  B.2 . 
 
 
 This factorization is used in the derivation of the marginal bound in Appendix  B.3 .
It does not assert that query and key activations observed at corresponding sequence positions are empirically independent. 
 
 
 
 B.2 Trace Normalization 

 
 Let 
 

 
 
 G Q ¯ = 𝔼 q ¯ ∼ 𝒟 Q ¯ ​ [ q ¯ ​ q ¯ ⊤ ] , G K = 𝔼 k ∼ 𝒟 K ​ [ k ​ k ⊤ ] 
 
 (8) 
 
 denote the second-moment matrices of the group-mean queries and keys. 
 
 
 We normalize the two marginals independently: 
 

 
 
 G ~ Q ¯ = G Q ¯ tr ⁡ ( G Q ¯ ) , G ~ K = G K tr ⁡ ( G K ) . 
 
 (9) 
 
 
 
 Equivalently, define 
 

 
 
 q ~ = q ¯ tr ⁡ ( G Q ¯ ) , k ~ = k tr ⁡ ( G K ) . 
 
 (10) 
 
 
 
 For a projector P , the exact score-error objective is 
 

 
 
 L sel ​ ( P ) 
 = 𝔼 q ¯ ∼ 𝒟 Q ¯ k ∼ 𝒟 K ​ [ ( q ~ ⊤ ​ ( I − P ) ​ k ~ ) 2 ] 
 
 
 
 
 = tr ⁡ ( ( I − P ) ​ G ~ Q ¯ ​ ( I − P ) ​ G ~ K ) , 
 
 (11) 
 
 which is the objective in Equation  3 of the main text.
It differs from the second moment of the unnormalized score error only by a constant factor: 
 

 
 
 L sel ​ ( P ) = 𝔼 ⁡ [ ( q ¯ ⊤ ​ ( I − P ) ​ k ) 2 ] tr ⁡ ( G Q ¯ ) ​ tr ⁡ ( G K ) . 
 
 (12) 
 
 
 
 Because the scaling factor is positive and independent of P , trace normalization does not change the minimizer of the exact selection objective.
It does, however, determine the relative weighting of the query and key marginals in the tractable surrogate.
For group-mean queries, we measure an average trace ratio of
 tr ⁡ ( G Q ¯ ) / tr ⁡ ( G K ) = 0.323 on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct.
Thus, the unnormalized sum G Q ¯ + G K is strongly key-dominated and shifts the resulting basis toward a key-reconstruction-like subspace.
Independent unit-trace normalization instead assigns equal total weight to the query and key marginals while preserving their internal spectral structure.

 (17)

 B.6 Comparison with Asymmetric Query–Key Factorization 

 
 KQ-SVD  ( Lesens et al., 2025 ) was originally proposed for KV-cache compression and derives separate low-dimensional query and key transformations to preserve attention scores.
To isolate whether this asymmetric score-preserving factorization is also effective for dynamic token selection, we adapt its factorization to JSSA’s scoring pipeline rather than using KQ-SVD as a compression method.
Specifically, we compute the KQ-SVD-style query and key projections from the same calibration activations used by JSSA and use them only to score candidate tokens; the original full-dimensional KV cache, token budgets, selection granularity, and sparse-attention execution remain unchanged.
The controlled comparison therefore primarily isolates JSSA’s shared orthonormal scoring subspace from an asymmetric query–key scoring factorization.

The asymmetric variant remains competitive in several settings, including closely matching JSSA on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct and Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512.
However, its performance is less consistent across architectures and context lengths, with a particularly large gap on Qwen3-14B.
These results show that the asymmetric factorization provides a viable low-dimensional scoring representation rather than a uniformly weak baseline.

For JSSA, projecting the newly generated key is negligible, accounting for at most 3.2% of pipeline latency and less than 1% at larger batches.
Top- B selection dominates at small batches, while the full-context score scan becomes increasingly important as the batch grows. 
 
 
 On Qwen3-8B, JSSA is faster than QUEST at every measured batch size, reducing pipeline latency from 2.69 ms to 1.52 ms at batch size 64.
On Gemma4-26B-A4B-It, the two methods are comparable at small batches, but JSSA becomes faster as the batch increases, reaching 0.91 ms versus 1.07 ms for QUEST at batch size 64.
QUEST’s page-scoring cost grows more rapidly with batch size, whereas JSSA benefits from its compact projected cache and regular score-scan kernel. 
 
 
 
 E.2 Projected Scoring Cache and Quantization 

 
 JSSA stores the auxiliary projected-key cache used for token scoring in FP8, while the original full-dimensional keys and values used by final attention remain in BF16.
To isolate the effect of scoring-cache quantization, we compare FP8 and BF16 projected-key caches on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-14B, and Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512 at the default projection rank r = 32 .
All other configurations, including the projection basis, token budget, and final attention computation, are held fixed. 
 
 
 Figure  10 reports average LongBench accuracy at a KV budget of 256 tokens.
FP8 and BF16 yield nearly identical results on all three models: the absolute difference is 0.18 points on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, 0.01 points on Qwen3-14B, and 0.05 points on Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512.
These results show that storing the projected scoring cache in FP8 has negligible impact on accuracy while reducing its memory footprint by half relative to BF16.

 E.3 Selection Granularity in GQA 

 
 In GQA, JSSA selects a distinct token set for each KV head using the mean of its associated query heads.
We compare this design with a head-shared alternative inspired by TokenSelect  Wu et al. (2025) , which aggregates head-specific token preferences through soft voting before forming a single token set for the entire layer.
This is stronger than naive unweighted sharing, but it still collapses distinct KV-head preferences into one shared selection. 
 
 
 Table  15 reports RULER accuracy under the same fixed-density budgets used in the main evaluation.
At 32K context, head-shared soft voting performs nearly identically to per-KV-head selection on all three models.
At 128K, however, per-KV-head selection is consistently better, by 5.29 points on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, 0.99 points on Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512, and 1.32 points on Qwen3-14B. 
 
 
 These results show that preserving distinct per-KV-head selections becomes important at long context.
To our knowledge, existing gather-free token-level kernels do not support such per-KV-head index sets under the standard GQA KV-cache layout.
JSSA’s indexed-attention kernel enables this more accurate selection granularity without physically gathering the selected keys and values.
